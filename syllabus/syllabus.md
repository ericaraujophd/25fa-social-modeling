Week 5 — Collective Behavior: Thresholds and Mobilization  

**Tue Sep 30**  
- **Session A:** *Collective behavior: from contagion to thresholds*  
  - Start with classical theories of crowd behavior: Le Bon (irrational contagion, “mob mind”), Blumer (collective excitement).  
  - Contrast with later critiques: individuals don’t just lose rationality; they act within social structures.  
  - Introduce Granovetter’s innovation: *thresholds* (the number of others acting before one decides to act). This provides a formal bridge between individual decisions and collective outcomes.  
  - Emphasize: thresholds → nonlinear outcomes (small differences in distribution → very different collective results).  

- **Session B (SRG):** Discussion of reading.  
  - Mark Granovetter, “Threshold Models of Collective Behavior” (*American Journal of Sociology*, 1978).  
  - Deliverable: SRG prep sheet due.  
  - **Plenary (10–15m):** Instructor-led synthesis highlighting key themes and guiding critical engagement.

————————————————————

**Thu Oct 2**  
- **Session A (Lab):** NetLogo threshold contagion model.  
  - Use “Simple Threshold Model” (from NetLogo Models Library or a simplified custom build).  
  - Students vary:  
    - Distribution of thresholds.  
    - Network structure (lattice vs. random connections).  
    - Seed size (how many start out “active”).  
  - Demonstrate: large-scale mobilization may depend on just a few low-threshold actors.  

- **Session B:** *Round-robin critique.*  
  - Rotate small groups, each tasked with a critique lens:  
    1. *Simplicity*: does the model capture enough?  
    2. *Complexity*: where does it overcomplicate?  
    3. *Missing dynamics*: what about norms, emotions, or leadership?  
    4. *Applications*: where would thresholds be most useful in real research?  
  - Deliverable: **Lab Memo #4** — describe an experiment with threshold variation + reflect on what the model reveals and misses.

**Thu Oct 16**  
- **Session A (Lab):** Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma in NetLogo.  
  - **Step 1: Introduce dilemma.** Present payoff matrix (T > R > P > S). Ask: where do we see this logic in real life?  
  - **Step 2: Run PD Two-Person Iterated.** Compare strategies (Tit-for-Tat vs. Always Defect, Always Cooperate vs. Defect, Grim Trigger). Observe long-run payoffs.  
  - **Step 3: Run PD Tournament.** Multiple strategies compete in a population. Students vary payoff matrix, initial mix of strategies. Observe which dominate.  
  - **Step 4: Debrief.** Why does Tit-for-Tat often succeed? What does this say about reciprocity and cooperation in society?  

- **Session B (Project Work):** *Scope Canvas.*  
  - Teams create a one-page scope document: research question, theoretical connection, proposed mechanisms/agents, potential outputs.  
  - Deliverable: project scope canvas (formative).  

**Deliverable:** **Lab Memo #4** — describe strategies tested, results (with screenshot), and reflection on insights about cooperation.

- **Session B (Seminar Debate):** *“Social media causes polarization.”*  
  - **Setup:** Assign half the class to “causes” and half to “reveals.” 10–15 minutes prep.  
  - **Format:**  
    - Opening Statements (5m): each side makes a short case.  
    - Evidence Round (10m): each side presents 2–3 pieces of evidence from readings/research.  
    - Cross-Examination (10m): moderated Q&A on whether polarization is driven by media, preexisting identities, or both.  
    - Closing Statements (5m): each side offers final remarks. Optional class poll on persuasiveness.  
  - **Plenary (10–15m):** Instructor synthesis that polarization is multi-mechanistic: echo chambers, affective sorting, and bounded confidence dynamics all contribute.
